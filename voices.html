Voices

Proto-Indo-European was last spoken about six thousand years ago, and nobody recorded it. We built it a voice. We made each sound in a simulated vocal tract, carried the results into two human voices, and trained a speech model on them. It now reads any line of PIE aloud.

Speak it

Write a line of PIE in the standard notation, or on a keyboard: h1 h2 h3 for the laryngeals, k' g' for the palatals, kw gw for the labiovelars, a slash after an accented vowel (h2o/wis). The model on the project's machine reads it aloud.

The sounds

The reconstruction calls for a set of sounds that no living language has kept together. There are three laryngeals. We say h₁ as a plain [h]. h₂ is a voiceless uvular fricative [χ], the scrape at the back of German Bach, and h₃ is its voiced, lip-rounded partner [ɣʷ]. The stops at the back of the mouth come in three rows: palatal [c ɟ], plain [k ɡ] and labiovelar [kʷ ɡʷ]. A labiovelar is one sound, with the lips already rounding as the stop releases. There are breathy-voiced stops [bʱ dʱ ɡʱ], and resonants that carry a syllable by themselves, like the l at the end of English bottle. A short vowel appears where a laryngeal stands between two consonants, so ph₂tḗr “father” is said [pə.téːr]. The accent is pitch: the accented syllable is sung higher.

Hindi has the breathy stops and Arabic has the uvular fricatives. Czech has syllabic r and l, Hungarian has palatal stops, and Swedish tells words apart by pitch. A speech engine trained on any one of these languages gets part of the inventory right and puts its nearest guess in place of the rest.

We took each phonetic value from the handbooks of Fortson, Ringe, Beekes, Clackson and Byrd. Where they disagree, we chose one reading and wrote down the others in the project's pronunciation notes. A converter turns written PIE into IPA by fixed rules, and everything that follows starts from its output.

What we tried first

We started with eSpeak-NG. It is a formant synthesizer: it builds speech by rule from a handful of resonances, and it can be taught new phonemes. We put together a PIE voice from its Hindi phoneme table, Arabic's pharyngeals and two level tones, then measured it. On h₂ówis the accented syllable sits at 98 Hz and the one after it at 82. Every sound landed where we had put it, in a voice nobody would mistake for a person.

When eSpeak meets a phoneme name it does not know, it drops the rest of the word and gives no warning. We caught it by reading each rendering back through eSpeak's own transcriber and comparing the result, symbol by symbol, with the IPA we had sent.

The neural models sound like people, and they cannot say half the inventory. Kokoro-82M, the best open model that reads IPA directly, has no symbol for breathy voice, lip rounding, a syllabic resonant or a high tone, and it drops what it does not recognize without a word. We ran 19 of its voices and eSpeak through the hard sounds, each in several spellings: 634 clips. The Hindi voices came closest, because Hindi had taught them breathy stops, yet they said the palatals as the affricates of Hindi च and ज. Commercial speech services accept IPA only within the sounds of each voice's own language.

We also tried keeping eSpeak's correct sounds and re-voicing them with a voice converter, which changes who seems to be speaking and leaves the words alone. The open converters kept the melody or the laryngeals, and seldom both.

A vocal tract in software

VocalTractLab, Peter Birkholz's articulatory synthesizer, is a three-dimensional model of a human vocal tract. Its tongue, lips, jaw and velum move toward target positions, with a model of the vocal folds beneath them and a pair of lungs beneath those. At each instant the program turns the shape of the tract into a branching tube of air and computes the sound from pressure and airflow, the way a throat makes it. It has no recordings to draw on, so it can make any sound a human vocal tract can make, including sounds no living language uses.

You write speech for it as a gestural score. The score has parallel tiers for the vowel, the lips, the tongue tip, the tongue body, the velum, the glottis, the pitch and the lungs. Each tier is a row of gestures, and each gesture is a target that the articulator approaches with its own time constant and then holds, so neighbouring sounds overlap and blend as they do in a mouth. VocalTractLab ships with a converter for German. We wrote our own compiler, pie_vtl, which turns PIE IPA straight into a gestural score, and we taught it every sound:

A closure, with the glottis set for short-lag voicing: unaspirated, as the reconstruction requires.
c ɟ
The tongue body raised against the hard palate, a closure shape added to each voice and tuned on its own anatomy.
kʷ ɡʷ
The velar closure, with the lips rounding into the release.
bʱ dʱ ɡʱ
A voiced closure released into murmur: the vocal folds vibrating with a gap left open behind them. The size of the gap was found by bisection, until a held murmured vowel sat a few decibels below the modal one.
χ
The tongue body at the uvula and the glottis wide, with a layer of noise shaped to a uvular fricative's spectrum laid over it, since the simulation alone makes little friction that far back.
h
An open glottis and breath, the noise filtered through VocalTractLab's own transfer function for the neighbouring vowel, so every h takes its vowel's colour.
r̩ l̩
A schwa held under the tongue-tip shape of [ɹ] or [l], long enough to carry the accent.
r
VocalTractLab's tongue goes where it is sent and cannot be blown into a trill, so a trilled r is built from contacts 40 ms apart, twenty-five a second, the rate of a real trill.

Timing and pitch came next. The simulated articulators lag behind the score, and stop releases came out 30 ms late until we moved the oral tiers 30 ms earlier. The accent is a rise on the pitch tier of about three semitones, as measured. Pitch drifts down across each line and drops two semitones at the end, and a question ends on a three-semitone rise. The male voice is VocalTractLab's JD3 speaker. The female voice is W02, from VocalTractLab 2.4, which has the newer model of the vocal folds.

We went through ten versions and measured each before anyone listened. Every render passes a gate. The accent has to lift at least 2.5 semitones, at least 97 percent of syllable nuclei have to be voiced, no vowel may fall more than 8 dB below the clip's median, and every χ and h has to carry its share of energy between 2 and 8 kHz.

Two human voices

The simulated voices are exact, and they have a faintly glassy timbre. To give them human voices, we ran each render through a speech-to-speech model, which re-voices a recording in another speaker's voice and keeps the timing to the millisecond. Every line went through twice, once into a male voice and once into a female one. The conversion damaged a few sounds. Converters treat a short burst of voiceless noise as background and remove it, so a word-final h, and sometimes a χ, could disappear. The converter also smoothed away the flutter of the trill and the breath after bʰ dʰ gʰ.

Because the timing survives conversion, we could repair those sounds exactly: our compiler records where every phone begins and ends. restore.py finds each h and χ the converter weakened and rebuilds it from noise shaped by the converted voice's own neighbouring vowel. In the Dawn hymn, the h at the end of the middle túH went from 0.00 to 0.57 of its energy in the 2–8 kHz band. retrill.py re-cuts the trill's contacts on the timing of each r, and murmur.py puts the breath back onto the release of every breathy stop.

A final gate set aside 746 clips that had a weak h or χ, a voiceless syllable nucleus or a flat accent. The other 3,369 clips became the training set, with 123 more held out for validation: 3.8 hours in the male voice and 4.3 in the female, at 24 kHz.

Teaching Kokoro

Kokoro-82M is an open text-to-speech model with 82 million parameters, released under the Apache licence and descended from StyleTTS 2. It reads phonemes, which is what let us teach it a language with no living speakers. Its symbol table had four unused slots. We gave them to breathy voice ʱ, rounding ʷ, the syllabic mark and the acute accent, so the model reads the pitch accent as a symbol like any other. Each training label is the converter's IPA for its clip, character for character, so the model learned exactly the alphabet it is sent today.

We trained it with the kikiri-tts recipe, in two stages of ten epochs each, on one GPU. The first stage teaches the model to rebuild the corpus audio. Its best epoch by validation loss started the second stage, which trains duration, pitch and style, the parts that make speech sound spoken. Each candidate epoch from the second stage read the held-out lines, was scored against the real clips on spectral distance, pitch error and duration, and then we listened. The tenth epoch became our voices, pie_m and pie_f. Each voice pack is drawn from 200 of its speaker's clips.

The voices at work

Today a line of PIE goes through the converter to IPA and then into Kokoro, which returns the audio along with the length of every phoneme. Those lengths place each word in time. The hymn to Dawn on the front page uses them to light each word as it is sung. The model runs on an ordinary processor, and we have used it to read every dictionary entry aloud in both voices.
