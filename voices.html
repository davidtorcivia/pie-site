Voices

Proto-Indo-European hasn't been spoken for roughly six thousand years. There is no recording, no writing, no speaker left. This is the story of giving a voice to a ghost language, rebuilt with modern technology so we can hear it spoken once more.

Speak it

Write a line of PIE in the standard notation, or on a keyboard: h1 h2 h3 for the laryngeals, k' g' for the palatals, kw gw for the labiovelars, a slash after an accented vowel (h2o/wis). The model on the project's machine reads it aloud.

The sounds

How do you piece together the sound of a language that has no speakers? This was the project's first hurdle. Strong scholarly work points to the probable pronunciations, but no descendant kept all the features of the original. There are three laryngeals: h₁, which I read (there is some debate) as a plain [h]; h₂, a voiceless uvular fricative [χ], the scrape at the back of German Bach; and h₃, its voiced, lip-rounded partner [ɣʷ]. The stops at the back of the mouth come in three rows - palatal [c ɟ], plain [k ɡ] and labiovelar [kʷ ɡʷ] (a labiovelar is one sound, the lips already rounding as the stop releases). Then there are the breathy-voiced stops [bʱ dʱ ɡʱ], resonants that carry a syllable all by themselves (like the l at the end of English bottle), a short vowel that turns up wherever a laryngeal sits between two consonants (so ph₂tḗr “father” comes out [pə.téːr]), and an accent made of pitch: the accented syllable is sung higher.

No single language has all of these features, but every one of them exists somewhere. Hindi has the breathy stops and Arabic the uvular fricatives, Czech says syllabic r and l, Hungarian has palatal stops, and Swedish tells words apart by pitch. Unfortunately, that means no speech engine built on living languages has the full inventory PIE needs (as the table below shows), so these sounds had to be built from scratch.

The phonetic values come straight from the handbooks of the field: Fortson, Ringe, Beekes, Clackson and Byrd. Where the authors disagree (in several places: on some level this is opinionated, speculative work), I picked one reading and recorded the others in the project's pronunciation notes. From there a converter turns written PIE into IPA by fixed rules, the same way every time. With that, we had what we needed to start building a spoken corpus.

Existing engines

When resurrecting a dead language, you need a baseline to compare against. This project started with eSpeak-NG, a formant synthesizer, which builds speech by rule out of a handful of resonances. Usefully, eSpeak can be taught new phonemes. For the PIE voice I borrowed eSpeak's Hindi phoneme table, Arabic's pharyngeals and a pair of level tones. Measured, it said exactly what it was told: every rendering, read back through eSpeak's own transcriber, matched the IPA it was sent, and on h₂ówis the accented syllable sits at 98 Hz and the one after it at 82. Listening to it, however, would have embarrassed Stephen Hawking. It was useful as a source of truth, but it could not tell us what the language really sounded and felt like.

Neural text-to-speech engines like Kokoro, which is fast and easy to experiment with, had the opposite problem: they sound like people, but they can only say the languages they were trained on. To see whether I could cobble something together, I ran 19 Kokoro voices, in several languages, through the hardest sounds in PIE, each in several spellings to help the model along - 634 clips in all. The Hindi voices came closest, since Hindi already has breathy stops, but they said the palatals as the affricates of Hindi च and ज, and some sounds were missing entirely. Commercial engines that accept IPA were no better: each is limited to the sounds of its own language.

None of this came close to the fidelity or accuracy I had imagined, so it was time for a new direction.

A vocal tract in software

The first usable voices in this project came from VocalTractLab, an articulatory synthesizer by Peter Birkholz. This software (and software like it, as we'll see) is a marvel. It is a full three-dimensional model of a human vocal tract, with a tongue, lips, jaw, velum, vocal folds and lungs, all of which can be moved precisely to simulate, physically, how a sound is made. The advantage is that you're working with primitives: there is no fixed set of sounds and no limit set by the languages a model was trained on, only your patience (or the number of tokens you have), your ear and your creativity. Any sound a human can make can be approximated.

VocalTractLab is driven by a gestural score, a set of parallel tiers for the vowel, the lips, the tongue tip, the tongue body, the velum, the glottis, the pitch and the lungs. Each tier is a row of gestures, and each gesture is a target the articulator moves toward with its own time constant and then holds, so neighbouring sounds overlap and blend the way they do in a real mouth. VocalTractLab is German software and ships with a converter for German (it turns typed German into a score), so I had Claude Opus write a compiler of my own, pie_vtl, which turns PIE IPA straight into a gestural score and knows every sound below.

p t k
A closure, with the glottis set for short-lag, unaspirated voicing.
c ɟ
The tongue body raised against the hard palate.
kʷ ɡʷ
The velar closure, with the lips rounding into the release.
bʱ dʱ ɡʱ
A voiced closure released into murmur, which is harder than it sounds: the vocal folds vibrate with a gap left open behind them. It took experimenting. The size of the gap was found by bisection, until a held murmured vowel sat a few decibels below the modal one.
χ
The tongue body at the uvula and the glottis wide, with a layer of noise shaped to a uvular fricative's spectrum laid over it (the harsh static you'll hear below), since the simulation alone makes little friction that far back.
h
An open glottis and breath, the noise filtered through VocalTractLab's transfer function for the neighbouring vowel.
r̩ l̩
A schwa held under the tongue-tip shape of [ɹ] or [l] and lengthened to carry the accent.
r
VocalTractLab's tongue cannot be blown into a trill, so a trilled r is built from contacts 40 ms apart.

So we have the primitives, but speech needs timing and cadence, and PIE's accent is pitch. The compiler handles both. For timing, each syllable is weighed as heavy or light (a heavy syllable gets two beats, a light one one), words are lengthened before the mid-line pause (the caesura) and at the end of a line, pauses go in at punctuation, and each word's sounds are stretched or squeezed to fit, vowels most and stops least. The simulated articulators lag behind the score, so the compiler moves them early: stop releases were landing 30 ms late until the mouth and velum tiers started 30 ms ahead. For pitch, it writes semitone targets on the pitch tier: a rise on every accented vowel (marked with an acute: á, ń̩), a slow fall across the line, and a further drop at its end, or a rise for a question. Two human voices

So we've got the right sounds, but it's a little like listening to someone speak underwater. The dream of this project was always natural speech, and that needs a genuinely human timbre, which meant there was no avoiding a neural model. Luckily there are open-source and commercial speech-to-speech models that re-voice a recording in another speaker's voice (so-called voice cloning, or deepfaking if you want to spark outrage), and the better ones keep the sounds, the pitch and even the timing to the millisecond. A small pilot showed that this could turn the VocalTractLab audio into human-sounding speech, though some accuracy was lost on the way. Converters treat a short burst of voiceless noise as background and tend to throw it away, so a word-final h or a χ could simply vanish. They also smooth things out: trills were lost and the breath after bʰ dʰ gʰ was cut short. It finally sounded like a person, but it was not quite PIE.

Repairs were possible, though. Because the timing survives conversion so closely, and the compiler records where every phone begins and ends, restore.py can find each h and χ the converter weakened and rebuild it. This worked better than I expected: in the Dawn hymn, the h at the end of the middle túH went from 0.00 to 0.57 of its energy in the 2–8 kHz band. Other tools followed: retrill.py re-cuts the trills, and murmur.py puts the breath back on the breathy stops. A lot could be fixed.

A final check found 746 clips with sounds still missing or a flat accent, and those were cut. In the end there were 3,369 clips ready for training: 3.8 hours in a male voice and 4.3 in a female one.

Teaching Kokoro

Kokoro-82M is a small, fast, open text-to-speech model with 82 million parameters. There are better models, faster models and more expressive models, but I love Kokoro: it's fun to play with and sounds great for what it is. It was also ideal for this work because it reads phonemes rather than letters, which is very convenient for a language with no writing and no living speakers. Even better, its symbol table had four unused slots, which became breathy voice ʱ, rounding ʷ, the syllabic mark and the acute accent, so the model reads the pitch accent as just another symbol. Every clip was labelled with its IPA, and everything was ready to go.

Training followed the kikiri-tts recipe in two stages of ten epochs each, on a single RTX 5090. The first stage teaches the model to rebuild the corpus audio (how to speak the language). The second adds adversarial training and teaches duration, pitch and style (how to sound good doing it). I picked the best epoch of each stage by having it read held-out lines and scoring the output against the real clips. The sixth epoch of the second stage won, and PIE had a voice. It was a huge step forward, but the accuracy wasn't where I wanted it: a phone recognizer misheard 73 percent of its sounds on held-out lines, where on recorded human speech the same recognizer misses about 45 percent. But luck was smiling on me.

A second vocal tract

VocalTractLab, as amazing as it is, has its limits. Browsing the web, I came across a project that has since gone viral: Chimera, by the programmer Jameson Hodge. Chimera does what VocalTractLab does, simulating the whole airway from the lungs through the vocal folds to the lips and nose, and improves on it across the board, thanks to fast, vibe-coded development. The sounds Jameson was getting out of it were far better than VocalTractLab's, so I forked it right away and set out to teach it PIE.

Chimera, like VocalTractLab, needed new sounds: a murmur source, a noise source at the back of the tongue for χ, and a fix for friction that was being lost where the pharynx meets the mouth. A planner written for the fork turns PIE IPA into Chimera's gestures, on the same timing and with the same pitch accent as pie_vtl, and then I set about measuring and tuning to see how close I could get. In the end the recognizer misheard about half as many of Chimera's sounds as VocalTractLab's (27 percent against 54, on the same held-out sentences), which, I hoped, would mean far better speech-to-speech.

A second corpus

The Chimera renders went through the same pipeline as the VocalTractLab ones, but this time I added two more voices, a second male and a second female, with about three hours each, in the hope that the variety would help Kokoro learn the language itself rather than one speaker. The new corpus came to 6,660 clips (the number was unintentional) and 16.4 hours. Clear sources make clear clips: on a shared set of test sentences the recognizer's error rate on the converted audio nearly halved, from 49 to 29 percent in the male voice and from 57 to 31 in the female.

Of course, there were still problems. The converter softens stop bursts, smooths away the flutter of a trill, weakens the breath after bʰ dʰ gʰ in the female voices and dulls their s. But once again, because the timing survived conversion and the planner records where every phone begins and ends, the remaining problems were easy to repair. After those fixes and a final check, the training corpus came to 6,491 clips and 16.1 hours, in four distinct voices.

The voices today

I ran another Kokoro training with the same recipe, this time ten epochs in stage one and fifteen in stage two. Nearly 11 hours of GPU time later, the final epoch, the fifteenth, scored best: the recognizer misses 57 percent of its sounds, where the previous model missed 73 and recorded human speech scores about 45. It also sounds great. It isn't perfect, but I'm reasonably confident that a genuine PIE speaker, assuming the reconstructions are right, could communicate with these voices. Of the four, the site uses the most accurate male and female, pie_m and pie_f. For a little added polish and sparkle, I trained a small network to restore the high frequencies (bandwidth extension), and every recording on this site has been through it.

The voices at work

Now I, or anyone with the models, can easily generate fairly accurate, natural-sounding PIE, simply by running a line through the IPA converter and into Kokoro and the bandwidth-extension model. Kokoro also returns the length of every phoneme, which is how the hymn to Dawn on the front page lights each word as it's sung. It all runs on an ordinary processor, and it lets a long-dead language speak once more.
