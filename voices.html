Voices

Proto-Indo-European hasn't been spoken in roughly six thousand years. There is no record, no writing, no speakers remaining. This is the journey of giving a voice to a ghost language rebuilt with sophisticated technology so we can hear it spoken once more.

Speak it

Write a line of PIE in the standard notation, or on a keyboard: h1 h2 h3 for the laryngeals, k' g' for the palatals, kw gw for the labiovelars, a slash after an accented vowel (h2o/wis). The model on the project's machine reads it aloud.

The sounds

How do you piece together a language that has no speakers? This was the initial hurdle of the project, and while we have strong scholarly work indicating probable pronunciations, the language has no descendants that kept all the features of the original. There are three laryngeals: h₁, which I read (as there is some debate) as a plain [h]; h₂, a voiceless uvular fricative [χ], the scrape at the back of German Bach; and h₃, its voiced, lip-rounded partner [ɣʷ]. Then there are stops at the back of the mouth which come in three rows - palatal [c ɟ], plain [k ɡ] and labiovelar [kʷ ɡʷ] (a labiovelar is one sound, the lips already rounding as the stop releases). Finally there are the breathy-voiced stops [bʱ dʱ ɡʱ], resonants that carry a syllable all by themselves (like the l at the end of English bottle), a short vowel that turns up wherever a laryngeal sits between two consonants (so ph₂tḗr “father” comes out [pə.téːr]), and an accent made of pitch (the accented syllable is sung higher).

While no one language carries all of these features, every one of these exists somewhere. Hindi has the breathy stops and Arabic the uvular fricatives, Czech says syllabic r and l, Hungarian has palatal stops, and Swedish tells words apart by pitch. Unfortunately this means no vocal synthesis program or model, which are trained off of existing languages, has the full inventory necessary to recreate PIE accurately (as you can see in the table below) - we'll have to recreate these sounds from scratch.

The phonetic values for this project come straight from the handbooks of the field: Fortson, Ringe, Beekes, Clackson and Byrd. Where the authors disagree (and there are several places, this is opinionated speculative work on some level), I picked one reading and recorded the others in the project's pronunciation notes. From there a converter turns written PIE into IPA via these fixed rules. Now we have what we need to start building a spoken language corpus.

Existing engines

When resurrecting a dead language, you need a baseline to compare against. This project started with eSpeak-NG, a formant synthesizer, which is a rule-based speech builder that constructs spoken language out of a handful of resonances (formants). Usefully, eSpeak can be taught new phonemes. For this PIE voice, I borrowed eSpeak's Hindi phoneme table, Arabic's pharyngeals and a pair of level tones. Running PIE written in IPA through the software and then measuring the output against phoneme recognition software showed that the voice was pronouncing correctly; listening to it, however, would have embarrassed Stephen Hawking. Clearly this was useful as a source of truth, but it could not give us a sense of what the language really sounded and felt like.

Neural text-to-speech engines, like Kokoro, which is fast and easy to experiment with, had the opposite problem: while they sound like humans, they only generate languages they already know from training on people speaking them. Just to see if I could cobble something together, I ran 19 different Kokoro voices in multiple languages through the toughest sounds in PIE (with different spellings and pronunciations to try and help the model out). Hindi ended up closest (it already has breathy stops), but its palatals sounded like the affricates च and ज and some sounds were missing entirely. I also looked for commercial engines that can handle IPA input, but they too were limited to the sounds already in a chosen language.

None of this was working at the fidelity or accuracy I imagined, so it was time for a new direction.

A vocal tract in software

The first generation of usable voices in this project started in VocalTractLab (VTL), an articulatory synthesizer by Peter Birkholz. This software (and software like it, as we'll see in a bit) is a marvel. It recreates a full three-dimensional model of a human vocal tract complete with tongue, lips, jaw, velum, vocal folds, lungs, etc., all of which can be precisely moved or controlled so as to physically simulate sound generation. The advantage to this is that you're working with primitives - there's no preprogrammed set of sounds or limits of the language a model is trained on, it's just your patience (or the number of tokens you have access to), your ear, and your creativity. Any sound a human can speak can be approximated.

VTL uses a gestural score for its interface, a set of parallel tiers for the vowel, the lips, the tongue tip, the tongue body, the velum, the glottis, the pitch and the lungs. Each tier is a row of gestures, and those gestures target the articulator, which moves or holds with its own time constant. This means neighbouring sounds overlap and blend the way they do in a real mouth. VTL is German software and ships with a converter for that language (it preconverts sounds you can type into the score for ease of use), so I had Opus write my own compiler, pie_vtl, which turns PIE IPA straight into a gestural score and knows every sound below.

p t k
A closure, with the glottis set for short-lag unaspirated voicing.
c ɟ
The tongue body raised against the hard palate.
kʷ ɡʷ
The velar closure, with the lips rounding into the release.
bʱ dʱ ɡʱ
A voiced closure released into murmur (harder than it sounds). The vocal folds vibrate with a gap left open behind them. This required experimentation and the size of the gap was found by bisection, until a held murmured vowel sat a few decibels below the modal one.
χ
The tongue body at the uvula and the glottis wide, with a layer of noise shaped to a uvular fricative's spectrum laid over it (the ugly static sound you'll hear below), since the simulation alone makes little friction that far back.
h
An open glottis and breath, with the noise filtered through VocalTractLab's transfer function for the neighbouring vowel.
r̩ l̩
A schwa held under the tongue-tip shape of [ɹ] or [l] and extended to carry the accent.
r
VocalTractLab's tongue cannot be blown into a trill, so a trilled r is built from contacts 40 ms apart.

So we've got the primitives, but PIE is a pitch language and to turn this into speech it needs timing and cadence and emphasis (which syllable is accented). The VTL compiler tackles both: for syllable weight it calculates heavy vs light, stretches words for the midline pause (caesura) or end of a line, adds pauses based on punctuation, stretches or squeezes vowels and consonants, and applies semitone targets on a pitch track based on acutes (á, ń̩). Two human voices

So we've got the right sounds, but it's a little like listening to someone speak underwater. The dream of this project was always for the speech to be natural and that requires a genuinely human timbre, which means there was no avoiding a neural model. Luckily, there are a variety of open source and for-profit speech-to-speech models available that will re-voice a recording into another speaker's voice (so-called voice cloning, or deepfaking if you want to spark outrage), and the better ones will do so with extreme accuracy in sounds, pitch, tone and even timing down to the millisecond. A small pilot proved that this was viable in taking the VTL audio and turning it into human-sounding speech, though some accuracy was lost in the process. Converters treat a short burst of unvoiced noise as background sound and tend to throw it away, so a word-final h or a χ would simply vanish. The converters also tend to smooth things out: trills were lost, breath after bʰ dʰ gʰ was cut early - it finally sounded like a person, but it was not quite PIE.

However, repairs were possible. Since timing is preserved so closely, and the compiler already records where each phone begins and ends, restore.py can use that data to find each h and χ the converter weakened and repair it. This was more successful than I expected: for example, in the Dawn hymn, the h at the end of the middle túH went from 0.00 to 0.57 of its energy in the 2–8 kHz band. Other tools like retrill.py would recut trills and murmur.py would reinsert breaths on breathy stops - there was a lot that was able to be fixed.

A final QC pass found some clips with persistent missing sounds, so those were cut, but in the end there were 3,369 clips, 3.8 hours of a male voice and 4.3 of a female, ready for training.

Teaching Kokoro

Kokoro-82M is a small, fast open text-to-speech model with 82M parameters. There are better models, there are faster models, there are more expressive models, but for whatever reason I love Kokoro: it's very fun to play with and sounds great for what it is. Kokoro was also ideal for the work here because it reads phonemes rather than letters, a very convenient fact for a language with no writing or living speakers. Even better, its symbol table has unused slots which became breathy voice ʱ, rounding ʷ, the syllabic mark and the acute accent, which means the model can read the pitch accent as just another symbol. The corpus was converted to IPA for each clip and everything was ready to go.

Training followed the kikiri-tts recipe in two stages of ten epochs each, on a single 5090. The first stage focuses on teaching the model to rebuild the corpus audio (how to speak the language) and then the second, which includes adversarial training, trains duration, pitch, and style (how to sound good while you do it). I ran 10 epochs on each stage and picked the best from each by having it read held-out lines and scoring the output against the held-out clips. Epoch 6 was the winner and PIE had a voice. It was a huge step forward, but the accuracy wasn't where I wanted it to be (73% misheard, when real human speech on the analyzer I was using is closer to 45%). But luck was smiling upon me.

A second vocal tract

VocalTractLab, as amazing as it is, is old software. I was browsing the web when I came across a project that has since gone viral called Chimera from programmer Jameson Hodge. Chimera did what VTL does, improved across the board thanks to rapid vibe-coded development. The sounds Jameson was getting out of it were far superior to VTL's, so I forked it right away and set out to teach it PIE.

Chimera, like VTL, required new sounds: a murmur source, a noise source at the back of the tongue for χ, and a fix for friction that was being lost where the pharynx meets the mouth. pie_vtl was adapted to convert PIE IPA into Chimera gestures, and then I set about measuring accuracy and tuning to see how close I could get it. In the end, Chimera ended up roughly twice as accurate at creating sounds as VTL was, which, I hoped, would mean FAR better speech-to-speech.

A second corpus

The Chimera renders were put through the same pipeline as the VTL output, but I decided to add two additional voices, one male and one female, at reduced corpus length in the hope that the variation would help Kokoro learn the language itself better. This new corpus is 6,660 clips (unintentional) and 16.4 hours long. It turns out that clear sources make clearer clips, and error rates on the newly converted audio were roughly half those of the old VTL methodology. The fix held up!

Of course, there were still problems. The converter softens stop bursts, smooths away the flutter of a trill, weakens the breath after bʰ dʰ gʰ in the female voices and dulls their s. But once again, because the timing survived the conversion, the planner makes it easy to repair and augment the small remaining issues. After these fixes and final QC, the training corpus ended up at 6,491 clips, 16.1 hours, in four distinct voices.

The voices today

I ran another Kokoro training session using the same recipe, this time with ten epochs in stage one and fifteen in stage two. Nearly 11 hours of GPU time later, the final epoch, 15, was scoring in the 50s against the recognizer (the previous model was in the 70s and real human speech is in the 40s) and was also sounding great. It's not perfect, but I feel reasonably confident that a genuine PIE speaker would, assuming our reconstructions are correct, be able to communicate with these voices. Of the four, the site uses the most accurate male and female, pie_m and pie_f. For a little added polish and sparkle, I trained a small net to restore high frequencies; this is present in all the audio clips on this site.

The voices at work

Now, I, or anyone with the models, can easily generate fairly accurate, natural-sounding PIE by simply running the language through the IPA converter and into Kokoro and the bandwidth extension model. It all runs on an ordinary processor and lets a long-dead ghost language speak once more.
