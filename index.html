roto-Indo-European

Proto-Indo-European is the ancestor of most languages spoken from Ireland to northern India. Spoken around 4000 BCE and never written down, every word we have is reconstructed from its descendants—marked with an asterisk (*). I rebuilt it as a working language: a lexicon, a grammar engine, a corpus written through it, a language model that reads and writes PIE, and voices trained to speak it.

c. 4000 BCE

Most linguists place the homeland on the Pontic–Caspian steppe, north of the Black and Caspian seas in what's now Ukraine and southern Russia. Its people herded cattle, sheep and goats, hauled solid-wheeled wagons behind oxen, and kept horses—their word for one was *h₁éḱwos. Whether they actually rode them is still debated.

Over the next two millennia their descendants spread west across Europe, south into the Balkans, Greece and Anatolia, and east through Central Asia to India, where the Rigveda would later be composed in Sanskrit. As communities drifted apart, their speech split into separate languages.

The oldest surviving writing in the family is Hittite: cuneiform clay tablets from Hattusa dating to the 1600s BCE. Each date on the map marks that branch's first written witness.

Nobody ever wrote down Proto-Indo-European itself. By the time anyone picked up a stylus, the original tongue had been gone for two thousand years.

Comparative reconstruction

Sounds change in regular ways—when a shift hits one word, it tends to hit dozens—so linguists can run the tape backwards. Take “father”: Vedic Sanskrit pitár, Ancient Greek patḗr, Latin pater, Gothic fadar. Line them up and they converge on a single older form, *ph₂tḗr. Every word in the lexicon carries that asterisk because it was worked out by comparison, never found carved in stone.

What reconstruction recovers least well is syntax: word order, how questions were framed, the little particles gluing clauses together. Pieces are solid—*-kʷe “and” tacked onto the end of a word, verbs tending to land last—while much of the rest is inferred from the oldest daughters and stays educated guesswork.

Method

None of this is unearthed text—it's PIE composed to obey the reconstructed grammar. Every record names its source: a scholar's published reconstruction, a shared poetic formula, a back-projection from parallel daughter texts, or a new passage built and checked by the engine.

Evaluation

Every model took the same held-out test—inflection, full paradigms, two-way translation, and dialogue—with no tools. Lenient scoring checks the grammar and forgives sloppy notation like missing accents or ASCII h2 for h₂. Strict scoring demands standard scholarly PIE. Frontier models hold up fine on lenient, then drop 25 to 37 points under strict. Mine drops 2.3.

A hymn to Dawn

A hymn from the corpus to *h₂éwsōs, the dawn goddess whose name became Vedic Uṣás, Greek Ēṓs and Latin Aurōra. It's read by Kokoro TTS models trained from scratch on PIE speech: first synthesized inside Chimera, a 3D physical model of the human vocal tract built to shape uvulars, palatals, breathy stops and pitch accent, then converted into natural human voices. “The sheep and the horses.” In 1868 August Schleicher wrote a short fable in the language he'd reconstructed—the first text ever composed in PIE. Almost every linguist since has tested their own reconstruction by rewriting it.
