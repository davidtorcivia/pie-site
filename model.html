Model

Proto-Indo-European has not been spoken for thousands of years, and nobody who spoke it wrote any of it down. Every PIE word we know today is a reconstruction. It is, however, the ur-language of many modern languages, and this thought was rolling around in my head while working with LLMs: the living language machines. Surely there is some echo within there? Wouldn't it be funny to pull it out? This is how I taught a language model to read and write PIE, in the scholars' notation.

The result

In the end, on the project's benchmark the model scores 88.5 lenient and 86.2 strict. The best frontier runs I tested, Claude Opus 5.5 at xhigh and GPT-6.1 Sol at high, score 86.0 and 84.4 lenient, then 50.0 and 47.0 strict. The lenient score forgives notation (accents, vowel length, ASCII spellings like h2 for h₂), and simply asks whether the grammar is right. The strict score grades the exact form, written the way the scholarly reconstructions write it. The frontier models lose over 35 points when the score demands this fidelity, while this model loses 2.3.

Generating this corpus was the most painstaking part of this process. There are no native speakers to learn from, no recordings and no text written by anyone who spoke the language. What we do have is a body of scholarship that infers and reconstructs each word from its descendants and then writes it in a notation full of laryngeals (h₁ h₂ h₃), three rows of back consonants (ḱ, k, kʷ), breathy-voiced stops, syllabic resonants and pitch accents. Remember, PIE was only ever spoken, and the academic sources still disagree about details of pronunciation, spelling and construction. A model can't pick that up from Common Crawl the way it picks up French - though I was surprised how well the frontier models did, largely because of reconstructed texts like Schleicher's. To really speak PIE though, it needed a lexicon, a grammar that inflects every word the same way every time, and a corpus written through that grammar.

The lexicon

The lexicon holds over 8,300 entries. Over 7,400 come directly from the well-sourced work of Wiktionary's reconstructions (roots, words and the derivatives in their descendant trees), over 800 are from LIV², the standard lexicon of PIE verbs, 135 are from the Nomina im Indogermanischen Lexikon and 3 were added by hand from scouring handbooks. LIV² and NIL don't exist as digital data, so I had them transcribed from page images: 1,172 roots from LIV², and 207 roots with 3,044 formations from all 737 pages of NIL. Where two transcriptions of the same page overlapped, they agreed 96% of the time.

A lexicon this size does carry errors (as do the sources), and the project made considerable efforts to find, correct, and source our corrections for these errors. 367 entries required fixes (mostly Wiktionary), usually a gloss that misled a writer or a stem the handbooks correct. When Wiktionary gave no meaning, 119 glosses were confirmed by the descendants themselves (the word's reflexes agree on a meaning in at least two branches), and 3,745 more were composed from the root's meaning and the suffix's function, marked as approximate so the reviews can measure them separately.

The grammar engine

The next step once the lexicon was built was to construct a grammar engine that could manipulate words, change their forms, and construct proper grammatically correct sentences automatically. The rules and exceptions were built from our academic source books and then, to verify, we had the engine rebuild Wiktionary's PIE inflection tables from scratch. It got 98.8% of the cells right, 14,023 of 14,197, and we traced the remaining mismatches to Wiktionary's own inconsistencies (such as a dropped laryngeal or an inconsistent delabialization), partial tables, or lexical exceptions the engine still needs an override for.

The engine works in two layers. The first takes a stem, its weak form and its accent class and builds cells: nouns in all eight cases and three numbers across the acrostatic, proterokinetic, hysterokinetic and amphikinetic classes, adjectives in three genders, and verbs through the present, aorist and perfect with subjunctive, optative, imperative, middle and participles. The second guesses the stem and class for the words Wiktionary lacks tables for, from the shape of the lemma. Every rule was backtested on the words that do have tables, with the table hidden, and only a rule that matches at least 85% of the hidden cells (remember, there are many errors on Wiktionary) may feed the corpus. A separate checker runs the lexicon forward through the sound laws into Vedic, Greek, Latin and Proto-Germanic as a warning system: 95% of 5,315 checks passed.

The corpus

The corpus is 5,230 records in four tiers with nearly all of it written through the engine. Tier 1 is the scholars' own text: 99 lines from 17 published versions of Schleicher's fable and "The King and the God", from Schleicher's 1868 original to Byrd, plus 7 reconstructed formulas from Watkins and West. Tier 2 is back-projection: 109 lines reconstructed from passages that the daughter traditions share (the Rigveda beside Homer, the Avesta beside the Edda), each with its attested witnesses. Tier 3 is the majority of the text and where the engine constructions came into play: 4,755 records, single sentences and connected texts, 127,896 words. There is also Tier 3b, 260 records of everyday talk, so the model can approximate something conversational.

It may be tempting to point a frontier model at this problem and say "write a corpus in PIE", but our Tier 3 intentionally avoids that methodology. Instead we had a model write an English text and a structured plan (which lexeme, which case or tense, which person), then the engine produces every form of those words, the model is allowed only to reorder the words and choose particles, and a validator checks the result, keeping only lines that pass. This was created in waves tackling different genres (epic, hymns, charms, laws, farm life, animal fables, speeches). We also included some coinages that modern speech needs, built from attested roots and productive suffixes only and logged with their derivations; they're 4% of the words in the training corpus.

Once a batch was completed, it would be passed to adversarial review with planted errors to check that the reviewer is paying attention. A review that finds a wrong record does not simply fix that record: it finds the cause in the engine, the classifier or the lexicon, fixes it there, and then the entire tier is regenerated.

Fine-tuning

We fine-tuned a number of models but settled on Nemotron 3.5 Super, NVIDIA's 120-billion-parameter mixture of experts (12 billion active), as our final base, scoring 88.5 lenient and 86.2 strict, first on the benchmark overall after fine-tuning was complete. Getting there took several model families, rounds of training, and plenty of experimentation.

The training data is the corpus turned into conversation turns (system prompts and all): 47,088 of them with a held-out test split of 713. About half are translations (what does X mean in English, or translate Y to PIE), the rest are dialogue in English and in PIE, interlinear glosses, single inflected forms, whole paradigms, reconstruction from cognates and the continuation of hymns and charms.

When first seeing if this was viable, I started small, with Qwen3-4B on a single RTX 5090, to see if it could learn PIE at all. The initial run was successful, though mediocre, but it validated that this was doable. I then tried several other small models to see what directions we could take: Qwen3-4B base (instead of the instruct-tuned version I initially used) and Typewriter, a model trained only on text from before 1913 (the logic being that it might be fun to have an older corpus for the model to work from). Neither panned out; both scored worse than the instruct-tuned 4B. It was time to scale up, so I moved to Qwen3.6-35B, trained on Tinker (my favorite quick training tool when I don't want to tie up my machines or when a model exceeds the size I have available): after one epoch it beat the more trained 4B, and it only scaled up from there. I spent some time RL'ing this new model, which was decently solid, but kept running into issues where it seemed like the model just wasn't memorizing or learning quite fully enough to grasp the nuances. Time to scale again. I reached for my favorite manageable fine-tuning family: Nemotron 3.5 Super.

Back on Tinker, I swept the learning rate and optimizer for Nemotron first (this was going to be a more expensive run with an expanded corpus). Thinking Machines' new Dimuon at 8e-3 won, 40 times the AdamW rate, and rank 32 seemed no better than rank 16. The full run went three epochs, 2,514 steps in about five hours. Interestingly, held-out loss bottomed in the first epoch and rose after it, which by the usual rule says stop at epoch one, but the third epoch won the benchmark, best on every segment except English → PIE, where it tied for first. For this corpus it turns out held-out loss isn't a stopping signal, so I'm glad I was scoring every epoch. Quantized to 5 bits to run on my Mac, it scores within noise of the full-precision model.

Reinforcement learning

I had never RL'd a model before, I don't know why, but I thought I'd give it a shot here. Reinforcement learning, with one short fine-tune in the middle, took the English words the model kept failing to translate into PIE from 78 to 3, and brought its English → PIE translation back to within a point of its best. I ran several rounds of RL, sometimes improving, sometimes not, sometimes merging different rounds when one area improved but another failed. At one point it became apparent there was a gap in the lexicon the model had learned (words in the lexicon that the training text rarely or never used, and of course the model can't know words it has never seen), so I trained those in with a quick local fine-tune and then RL'd one more round after that. The end result is the model here today.

All of the RL'ing ran on my Mac Studio, with GRPO in mlx-lm-lora. For each prompt the model samples eight answers, and the ones that score above their group's mean are reinforced. The reward is local wherever the engine can decide. Inflections and paradigms are checked against the engine's cells; a translation scores zero for an empty answer, commentary, padding or any word the engine doesn't know, and one for a reference answer. Only what the local checks can't settle reaches a Claude judge: Haiku 5.5 at high effort screens (surprisingly, Haiku outscored Opus at catching planted errors in my tests), and a second Haiku call re-checks what the first flagged. An answer counts as wrong only if both say so.

The final round still trails the round before the vocabulary fine-tune by about a point of chrF and a little on paradigm cells. I tried blending the two sets of weights exactly (stacking the two adapters, so the result is a weighted sum of what each one changed), but every blend traded the new words against the grammar, and none beat the final round outright.

Measuring it

Of course, when you're training you need some way to measure improvement, so I constructed a number of benchmarks that were refined into the scoring system you see on this site now. When tuning, every checkpoint was measured on prompts held out of all training. The benchmark combines automatic scores as well as judging (more on that in a moment). The auto scores are chrF against the reference, exact match on single forms, the share of a paradigm's cells the model gets right, and the share of its PIE words the engine recognizes as real forms. They're quick and automatic and helpful for catching regressions when training.

However, because they are simple, they can also mislead. chrF against one reference punishes a different but correct translation, so the benchmark has the translations and dialogue judged by Claude Opus 5.5 and by GPT-6.1 Sol, two vendors, so no vendor grades its own models alone, each with the engine and the corpus as tools and the reference beside the answer. Each item scores 1, 0.5 or 0, and separately for notation. Models answer from their weights alone with no tools or access to any data. The end result is the benchmark you see here!
