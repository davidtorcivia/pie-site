Model

Proto-Indo-European has not been spoken for thousands of years, and nobody who spoke it wrote any of it down. Every PIE word we know today is a reconstruction. It is, however, the ur-language of many modern languages, and this thought was rolling around in my head while working with LLMs: the living language machines. Surely there is some echo in there? Wouldn't it be funny to pull it out? This is how I taught a language model to read and write PIE, in the scholars' notation.

The result

On my held-out benchmark, my top model, Wekwos-2-Mini (trained on Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini with engine-verified chain of thought and GRPO RL), scores 90.5 lenient and 88.2 strict. It edges past my earlier 120B flagship, Wekwos-120B (Nemotron 3.5 Super, 88.5 lenient and 86.2 strict), at a fraction of the size. The strongest frontier runs I tested (Claude Opus 5.5 at xhigh and GPT-6.1 Sol at high) hit 86.0 and 84.4 lenient, then drop to 50.0 and 47.0 strict. Lenient scoring ignores notation (accents, vowel length, ASCII spellings like h2 for h₂) and only checks the grammar. Strict scoring requires the actual scholarly form. Frontier models lose more than 35 points under strict grading; mine lose 2.3.

The hard part was building the training data. There are no native speakers, no recordings, and no ancient texts to scrape, only a century and a half of comparative scholarship written in a dense notation: three laryngeals (h₁, h₂, h₃), three rows of back consonants (ḱ, k, kʷ), breathy-voiced stops, syllabic resonants, and pitch accents. Because PIE was only ever spoken, even the handbooks disagree on details of pronunciation and spelling. A model can't pick that up from Common Crawl the way it picks up French. I was honestly surprised how well frontier models did on lenient grading (mostly thanks to famous reconstructions like Schleicher's fable floating around in pretraining), but getting a model to write clean, consistent PIE took a proper lexicon, a deterministic grammar engine, and a corpus built through both.

The lexicon

The lexicon holds 8,368 entries: 7,401 from Wiktionary's PIE reconstructions (roots, words, and descendant-tree derivatives), 829 verbal roots from LIV², 135 nouns from the Nomina im Indogermanischen Lexikon (NIL), and 3 pulled out of handbooks by hand. Neither LIV² nor NIL exists as clean digital data, so I had both transcribed from page scans (1,172 roots from LIV², plus 207 roots and 3,044 formations across all 737 pages of NIL). Where two transcriptions of the same page overlapped, they agreed 96% of the time.

Any lexicon this size has mistakes, and so do its sources. I patched 367 entries, mostly from Wiktionary, fixing misleading glosses or stems the handbooks disagree with. Where Wiktionary gave no meaning at all, 119 glosses were pinned down from daughter reflexes that agree across at least two branches, and another 3,745 were derived from root plus suffix (flagged as approximate so reviews could track them separately).

The grammar engine

Once the lexicon was in shape, I needed a grammar engine that would inflect words deterministically, producing the same forms from the same rules every time. I encoded the morphology from the standard handbooks and tested it by regenerating Wiktionary's PIE inflection tables from scratch. It matched 98.8% of the cells (14,023 of 14,197), and the remaining misses traced back to Wiktionary's own typos (dropped laryngeals, inconsistent delabialization), incomplete tables, or genuine irregulars that needed an override.

The engine runs in two layers. The first takes a stem, its weak form, and its accent class and builds every cell: nouns across eight cases, three numbers, and four accent classes (acrostatic, proterokinetic, hysterokinetic, amphikinetic); adjectives in three genders; verbs across present, aorist, and perfect with subjunctive, optative, imperative, middle, and participles. The second infers stem and class from lemma shape alone for words without Wiktionary tables. Every classifier rule was backtested with real tables hidden, and only rules clearing 85% (remember, Wiktionary itself is noisy) were allowed into the corpus. A separate forward checker pushes the lexicon through sound laws into Vedic, Greek, Latin, and Proto-Germanic as a sanity check: 95% of 5,315 checks passed.

The corpus

The corpus is 5,230 records across four tiers, nearly all of it built through the engine. Tier 1 is the scholars' own text: 99 lines from 17 published versions of Schleicher's fable and “The King and the God” (from Schleicher's 1868 original up to Byrd), plus 7 reconstructed poetic formulas from Watkins and West. Tier 2 is back-projection: 109 lines reconstructed from parallel phrases shared across branches (Rigveda beside Homer, Avesta beside the Edda), each tied to its attested witnesses. Tier 3 makes up the bulk of the text, with 4,755 engine-built sentences and connected passages (127,896 words). Tier 3b adds 260 records of everyday talk so the model can actually hold a conversation.

If you point a frontier model at a blank prompt and ask for PIE, you get plausible-looking gibberish. Tier 3 splits the job up: a planner model writes the English along with a structured grammatical plan (lexeme, case, tense, person), the engine produces every inflected form, the planner arranges word order and particles, and a validator drops anything that fails. I built it out in genre waves (epics, hymns, charms, laws, farm life, animal fables, speeches), alongside a small set of modern coinages built strictly from attested roots and productive suffixes with every derivation logged (about 4% of training words).

Every finished batch went through adversarial review with deliberate errors planted inside to make sure the reviewer was paying attention. When a real bug turned up, I fixed the root cause in the engine, classifier, or lexicon instead of hand-patching the line, then regenerated the whole tier.

Fine-tuning

I scaled up through several bases before landing on my first flagship, Wekwos-120B (built on Nemotron 3.5 Super, NVIDIA's 120-billion-parameter mixture of experts with 12B active). After fine-tuning it hit 88.5 lenient and 86.2 strict, holding first place on the benchmark until a much smaller reasoning model caught up and passed it.

The corpus was exported into 47,088 conversation turns (system prompts included), with 713 held out for testing. Roughly half are translations in both directions; the rest cover English and PIE dialogue, interlinear glosses, single inflections, full paradigms, cognate-to-PIE reconstruction, and continuing hymns or charms.

To see if any of this was even viable, I started small with Qwen3-4B on a single RTX 5090. That first run was mediocre, but it proved a model could learn PIE. Next I tried Qwen3-4B base (without instruct tuning) and Typewriter, a model trained strictly on pre-1913 English, because I liked the idea of starting from an older linguistic world. Both scored worse than the instruct-tuned 4B. From there I scaled up to Qwen3.6-35B on Tinker (my go-to when I don't want to tie up local GPUs or when a model outgrows my hardware). After a single epoch it beat the longer-trained 4B and kept climbing. A round of RL made it fairly solid, yet it still hit a ceiling on the finer morphological rules. So I scaled up one more time to my favorite manageable fine-tuning family: Nemotron 3.5 Super.

Back on Tinker, I swept learning rates and optimizers first since this run would cost more on the expanded corpus. Thinking Machines' Dimuon at 8e-3 won (40× the AdamW rate), and rank 32 didn't beat rank 16. The full run went three epochs: 2,514 steps in about five hours. Weirdly, held-out loss bottomed out in epoch 1 and climbed after that, which by the textbook rule means you stop at epoch 1. Yet epoch 3 won the benchmark, taking first on every segment except English → PIE (where it tied). Held-out loss turned out not to be a reliable stopping signal here, so I'm glad I benchmarked every epoch. Quantized to 5 bits to run locally on my Mac, it lands within noise of full precision.

Reinforcement learning

I had never RL'd a model before (I don't know why, I just hadn't), so I figured I'd give it a shot here. Several rounds of RL, plus one short vocabulary fine-tune in the middle, cut the English words the model consistently failed to translate into PIE from 78 down to 3, and brought its English → PIE score back within a point of its peak. Some rounds helped, some regressed, and a few times I merged adapters when one skill climbed while another slipped. Midway through it became obvious there was a vocabulary blind spot: lexicon words that rarely showed up in the training text (and a model can't guess PIE words it has never seen). A quick local fine-tune taught those words, followed by one last round of RL.

All the RL ran locally on my Mac Studio using GRPO in mlx-lm-lora. For each prompt the model samples eight completions, reinforcing whichever beat the group mean. Rewards stay local whenever the engine can grade them: inflections and paradigms check directly against engine cells; translations get zero for an empty reply, chatter, padding, or any unknown word, and one for a reference match. Only what local rules can't settle goes to a Claude judge: Haiku 5.5 at high effort screens first (surprisingly, Haiku beat Opus at catching planted errors in my tests), and a second Haiku call double-checks anything flagged. An answer only loses credit if both agree.

The final round on Nemotron still trailed the pre-vocab checkpoint by about a point of chrF and a sliver on paradigm cells. I tried blending the two adapters directly (taking a weighted sum of their weight deltas), but every ratio traded the new vocabulary straight off against grammar, and none beat the final round outright.

Then Thinking Machines released Inkling 2 Mini, a 26.4-billion-parameter looped mixture-of-experts model (3.4B active per token, Apache 2.0) with a native thinking channel (24 layers, 128 routed experts with 8 active, looping its 12-layer core three times) that is 4.5× smaller than Wekwos-120B and runs comfortably on a 32 GB laptop. Plain direct fine-tuning on the same corpus only reached 76.9 strict (79.6 lenient), well behind the 120B model. The unlock was teaching it to actually use its scratchpad for PIE morphology: I generated 58,040 engine-verified chain-of-thought examples that decompose every English → PIE sentence into sense, lemma, grammatical features, and surface form, write out stem and ending deltas for inflections, and disambiguate homophones before answering. That single change jumped Inkling 2 Mini by 8.5 points to 85.4 strict (89.2 lenient).

From that checkpoint, two rounds of GRPO reinforcement learning on Tinker (with 100% deterministic, zero-API rewards from the grammar engine and analyzer: 7,650 broad prompts in round 2, then 4,313 prompts focused on English → PIE and dialogue in round 3) pushed it past the 120B model to 88.2 strict and 90.5 lenient: 90.4 on inflection, 89.6 on paradigms, 85.7 strict on English → PIE (up 4.7 points over Wekwos-120B), 89.3 on PIE → English, and 87.5 on dialogue. That checkpoint is Wekwos-2-Mini, published on Hugging Face in MLX, GGUF, BF16, and LoRA.

Measuring it

You can't train blind, so I built a suite of held-out evals early and gradually turned them into the benchmark on this site. Every checkpoint ran on prompts kept strictly out of all training. The automatic metrics (chrF against a reference, exact match on single forms, paradigm cell accuracy, and the share of PIE words recognized by the engine) are fast and good for catching regressions mid-run.

Automatic metrics only tell part of the story, though. Single-reference chrF punishes a valid translation that happens to pick a synonym or different word order. So translations and dialogue are also graded by two judges from different labs (Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Sol, so neither vendor grades itself alone), each given the grammar engine, the corpus, and the reference answer. Every item is scored 1, 0.5, or 0 for meaning and grammar, and graded separately for scholarly notation. Contestant models answer from weights alone, with no tools or lookup. That's the benchmark you see here.
